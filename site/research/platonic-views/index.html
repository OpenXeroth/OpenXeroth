OpenXeroth / Research / Platonic views
Satellite representations · Research in preparation

Different sensors.
A similar picture of the world?

A radar satellite and a camera see the same landscape in very different ways. Can two AI systems, trained separately, learn to agree about which places are alike? We found that changing which observations they learn to pair makes a measurable difference.

Graham Wallington & Jackie Lighten · Xeroth AI Limited · Manuscript revision 1.3 · 10 November 2026

01 / The bigger question

What is a world model?

You can recognise a road in sunshine or rain, and imagine what might happen if a car turns into it. A useful world model would give an AI system an internal description of its surroundings that helps it predict what happens next, including the effects of actions.

A representation is one ingredient: a compact description of an observation, stored as numbers. It can preserve useful properties while leaving out details. A map, for example, keeps the road layout without drawing every leaf.

Our research asks what makes two independently learned representations more alike. This could inform the representations used in world models. We did not train or test a system that forecasts events, plans actions or simulates the world.

02 / Two ideas behind the experiment

Why might separate models agree?

01

The Platonic Representation Hypothesis

Imagine two people drawing maps of the same town from different walks. Their maps may look different, yet place the same streets near one another. Huh and colleagues proposed that AI models can also come to organise observations in increasingly similar ways as they grow. The “Platonic” idea is that different observations might lead towards a shared statistical description of the world. It is a hypothesis, with limitations and counterexamples. Read the original paper ↗

We kept model size and training budget fixed. Our experiment asks about training pairs, rather than whether larger models converge.

02

Good views and the theorem behind them

When a model is taught that two pictures belong together, it has a reason to keep what they share. Two crops of one photograph can share both the scene and the quirks of that exposure. Two separate observations may share the scene while changing the lighting or measurement noise. Those can encourage different kinds of similarity.

Balestriero and LeCun’s Platonic Representation Theorem gives conditions under which separately trained models learn the same arrangement of world states, allowing for a rotation of their numerical coordinates. Their good-view analysis explains why independently varying observation quirks can help. It depends on idealised assumptions about the observations, the pairing relationships and the learning objective. Read the theorem and good-view analysis ↗

Real satellite passes also change season, viewing geometry and sometimes the ground itself. We tested an empirical prediction inspired by this theory; we did not establish its assumptions or verify its mathematical bound.

03 / The experiment

Same images.
Different partners.

We trained one set of models on Sentinel-1 radar and another on Sentinel-2 optical imagery. Each model learned only from its own sensor. Within each sensor and random seed, the two training methods used the same starting weights, sampled images, crop windows and training budget. We changed which crops were paired.

Each step supplied two crops from date A and two from date B, all of the same place. Switch the diagram to see the two pairing rules.

The encoders used the same ResNet-50 architecture and LeJEPA learning method. We compared a simple crop recipe and a stronger recipe that also changes scale, orientation and band values.

04 / How we measured agreement

Do they choose similar neighbours?

For each test place, we asked each model for the ten places it considered most similar within its geographic block. We measured how much the two lists overlapped. Higher overlap means the models organise those places more similarly. It does not mean the radar and optical pictures look alike.

This comparison uses each model’s own representation, with no fitted conversion between the sensors. Test geography was kept out of training.

We also tested whether a radar observation could find the optical observation of the same place on another date. In that original test, repeat-observation training increased the fraction of matched alternatives beaten by 24.9 percentage points under stronger augmentation (95% geographic interval: 21.4–28.1). This is a ranking score, rather than a percentage of places identified correctly.

06 / What the result means

What you keep depends
on what you ask to stay the same.

Recognising a place across dates rewards stability. Monitoring that place can require sensitivity to change. After repeat-observation training, a simple linear readout recovered less of the optical vegetation-index change: its R² score fell from 0.927 to 0.752. That score describes how well this particular readout explained the measured change; it does not show that the model lost all information about change.

01

The pairing comparison bundles several effects.

The same-observation crops shared at least 76.6% of their area before augmentation, including repeated measurements of the same pixels. Repeat observations change both those measurements and date-related conditions. We have no control with disjoint same-observation crops, so we cannot say which part caused the gain.

02

Shared structure can be quite ordinary.

Land cover and terrain can make places look similar to both sensors. More agreement does not establish a shared understanding of physics, or that the models encode every place’s unique identity. We tested one model family at one training budget.

03

The evidence is about representations.

The result supports a practical conclusion: choosing which observations belong together can change what independently trained models share. Whether that helps prediction, planning or a particular monitoring task needs its own test.
