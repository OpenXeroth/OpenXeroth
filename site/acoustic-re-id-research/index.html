Acoustic re-identification research

Can AI be used to identify
individual animals accurately?

Previous studies suggest AI can recognise individual animals from their calls. We found that recording conditions and the way recordings are divided for testing can inflate that apparent accuracy. Our study examines how much the background, microphone and recording session contribute to the result.

The question

What could individual recognition make possible?

Following the same animal over time could help researchers study survival, movement, social relationships and responses to environmental change. Recognising a familiar caller could extend that record beyond what a camera can see, with less need to catch, tag or disturb animals.

Earlier studies have approached this with machine learning (ML): computers learn patterns from labelled recordings and use them to suggest which animal made a new call. The promise is considerable, but a useful system must recognise an animal in a different session, with a different background, and when unfamiliar animals arrive.

Imagine recognising a friend on the phone. Their voice should remain familiar when they call from a busy street or a different handset. In animal recordings, a microphone or background can instead become an accidental name tag. We tested how much that affects the evidence for individual recognition.

What we tested

Four ways to check
if the computer got it right.

01

Change the recording session.

The unit being divided matters. Training on some of Monday’s calls and testing on other calls from Monday can share background and recording conditions. Training on Monday and testing on Tuesday asks whether the model recognises the bird in a new session. We compared these designs using the same recordings and the same training and test counts for each bird. Across 18 comparisons, three species and ten fixed random splits, randomly dividing individual clips raised measured accuracy by 20–51 percentage points. That higher score does not show improved recognition on a new day. Whole sessions can themselves be assigned randomly while keeping their clips separate.

02

Take the animal’s voice away.

Could the system still predict the assigned identity from background alone? For chiffchaff recordings within a year, ordinary accuracy was 73.7% from backgrounds versus 76.5% from calls. Giving every bird equal weight produces balanced accuracies of 71.4% and 79.1%. The background result remains substantial, although the two scores are not equivalent. A high score can carry information about the recording context as well as the animal.

03

Hold the microphone and recording constant.

Where several animals shared a recording, we could ask whether the model distinguished them beyond that shared context. Of three datasets with this stronger test, only one zebra finch dataset gave clear evidence of identity information beyond the recording. Timing is also a limitation: the two BirdPark datasets contain no same-bird pairs from different recordings within seven days. They cannot provide a same-day check that separates recording changes from changes over time.

04

Let strangers arrive.

In nature, a caller may be an animal the system has never met. We set an acceptance threshold using separate unfamiliar animals, targeting no more than one in ten strangers. We then tested two ways of making a match: comparing a call with each familiar bird’s average sound profile, or with its closest individual training call. On wild datasets separated by session, the highest median proportion of familiar calls accepted and correctly named rose from 37% with average profiles to 46% with nearest-call matching. These are results for the tested methods, not limits on what future models can achieve. The median is over different assignments of birds to test roles, and measures calls rather than individual animals. Actual stranger acceptance could exceed the target; using more calibration strangers did not consistently solve this.

These are specific benchmark results, not forecasts of Djuma performance. See the paper for uncertainty intervals, dataset qualifications and the complete comparisons.

The study at a glance

Four checks, with their limits.

One further limitation remains: the added-background experiment used a single fixed assignment of background donors. Testing several donor assignments would be a separate robustness experiment.

Why it matters

Better tests.
More trustworthy tools.

We found no neural network that won on every dataset. Choosing a different internal layer also changed the rankings. The useful lesson is to test the entire recognition process against the conditions it will actually face.

For NatureCam and Hosana, that means treating individual identity as a claim to validate. Today’s BirdNET-Cloud detections at Djuma are species suggestions. They do not establish how many individual birds are present.

The study uses existing published datasets. It does not use Djuma Cam audio as a benchmark or demonstrate a working individual bird recogniser at Djuma.
