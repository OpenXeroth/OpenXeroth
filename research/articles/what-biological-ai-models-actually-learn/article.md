# What biological AI models actually learn

Across bioacoustics, ecology, plant science, neuroscience, medicine and chemistry, machine-learning models reported as near-perfect have repeatedly turned out to be recognising where, when and with what a measurement was taken, rather than the organism itself. This article sets out the evidence field by field, from the primary sources, and the checks that expose it.

## Every biological measurement carries two signals

A recording of a bird, a voltage trace from a plant stem, a pathology slide or a chest X-ray holds two things at once. The first is the organism: the shape of the call, the pattern of the electrical signal, the structure of the tissue. The second is the context in which the measurement was made: the background sound at that site, the electrode and its contact with the stem, the staining batch at that hospital, the model of scanner, the time of day and the week of the year.

The biological signal is usually weak, variable and high-dimensional. The context signal is usually strong, stable within a session or site, and simple. A classifier trained to separate labelled groups has no idea which of the two it is supposed to use. It uses whichever separates the labels most easily. Geirhos and colleagues (2020) call this shortcut learning, and borrow a phrase from linguistics to describe it, the principle of least effort: networks adopt the easiest decision rule that works on the training data, whether or not that rule has anything to do with the thing the labels name.

The problem arises whenever the experimental design lets the label of interest line up with the context. If each individual animal is recorded at its own site, each disease cohort is photographed on its own day, or each patient group is scanned at its own hospital, the context predicts the label perfectly, and the model has no reason to learn anything harder. Lapuschkin and colleagues (2019) called models that do this Clever Hans predictors, after the horse that appeared to do arithmetic by reading its trainer's posture. Their best-known example was an image classifier that recognised horses by a source tag, a small text label in the lower-left corner of about a fifth of the horse photographs in a standard benchmark; removing the tag removed the horse, and pasting it onto a car turned the car into a horse.

This is a general failure, documented across science. Kapoor and Narayanan (2023) collected cases of data leakage, where information from the test data reaches the model during training, across 17 fields and 294 papers, and showed in their own re-analysis of civil-war prediction that once leakage was corrected the complex models performed no better than logistic regression.

What follows is a field-by-field account of this failure in the life sciences, where it takes particular forms: the territory that stands in for the bird, the studio backdrop that stands in for the leaf disease, the time of day that stands in for the mental image, and the hospital that stands in for the tumour. Every figure below comes from the paper cited beside it, and the full list of sources with links is at the end.

## Wildlife: the territory stands in for the animal

In the field, the identity of a calling animal is rarely established by catching it. It is inferred from where the recording was made. A territorial songbird is recorded on its territory, a nesting seabird at its nest, a tagged whale by the tag it is wearing, a camera-trap animal at the camera that photographed it. Each of these assigns identity by place or device, and so ties every example of an individual to one set of recording conditions.

[Stowell, Petrusková, Šálek and Linhart (2019)](https://doi.org/10.1098/rsif.2018.0940) tested what that does to a classifier, in chiffchaffs, tree pipits and little owls recorded on their own territories. They trained individual-recognition classifiers on the birds' songs and then applied them to ambient recordings from the same places containing no target bird. If the classifier had learned the bird, it should have been at chance on the empty recordings. For chiffchaffs within a single year it was not: it identified the "right" bird from background sound alone at close to 70% AUC. The same classifiers scored above 85% AUC on chiffchaff songs within a year, and around 60% when trained in one year and tested in the next, after the territories' sound had changed.

[Tolkova, Klinck, Cusano, Kügler and Parks (2026)](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pcbi.1013321) found the same thing in North Atlantic right whales recorded on suction-cup tags. Because each whale wore its own tag for one deployment, whale identity and tag recording were the same variable. BirdNET embeddings with a simple classifier identified the 11 whales from their upcalls with 84% accuracy; the same pipeline applied to one second of water noise taken just before each call, with no whale in it, scored 79%. Only after aggressive de-noising did the noise-only accuracy fall to 24%, near chance, while the call accuracy held at about 66%. The authors reported both numbers.

Camera traps have the same structure. [Beery, Van Horn and Perona (2018)](https://arxiv.org/abs/1807.04975) trained species classifiers on images from fixed camera stations and tested them on new images from the same stations and on images from stations never seen in training. Top-1 error more than doubled, from 19% at familiar stations to 41% at new ones. Each camera sees one patch of rock, trunk and vegetation, and species are not evenly distributed across cameras, so the background predicts the animal. The widely reported figure of 93.8% to 94.9% species accuracy on the Serengeti dataset ([Norouzzadeh et al. 2018](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1719367115)) was obtained with training and test sets split by capture event, not by camera location.

The splitting problem appears wherever one recording yields many clips. [Arnaud and colleagues (2023)](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pcbi.1010325), working on bonobo calls, compared the usual random assignment of calls to training and test sets against one that kept calls from the same vocal sequence together, and found in one of their comparisons a 12.5 percentage-point gap in balanced accuracy, concluding that "performance is clearly overestimated when the classifier can extract information that would be inaccessible in real life conditions".

How common are these designs? [Knight and colleagues (2024)](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.tree.2024.05.007) reviewed 598 studies of individual acoustic signatures across all animal groups, 358 of which trained classifiers to identify individuals. The mean number of individuals used to train and test a model was 20.3. Most, 93.3%, used targeted recording rather than passive recorders; and 93.3% used closed-set classification, in which every test call is guaranteed to come from one of the enrolled animals. Only 6.7% tested the open-set case, where strangers are present. The field's own methods paper for deep learning on animal sound ([Ghani, Baier, Kalkman and Stowell 2026](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pcbi.1014604)) now says plainly: "Avoid purely random dataset splits, as they are prone to site- or time-based leakage" and "models may overfit to site-specific background noise rather than learning generalisable species characteristics".

None of this means individual recognition from sound is impossible. A 2025 preprint from the same group as the Knight review ([Lapp et al. 2025](https://doi.org/10.1101/2025.06.26.661638)) reports identifying individual ovenbirds with 96% accuracy, with no audio or individuals from any test array included in training, and uses the method to follow 405 birds over four years. It means that the accuracy figure on its own does not tell you whether the model has learned the bird or the place.

## Plants: the backdrop stands in for the disease, the week stands in for the stress

**Leaf images.** PlantVillage is the standard benchmark for crop-disease recognition: 54,306 photographs of leaves in 38 crop-and-disease classes, taken against plain backgrounds. [Mohanty, Hughes and Salathé (2016)](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpls.2016.01419) trained networks that reached 99.35% on held-out PlantVillage images. On two small sets of images gathered from other sources (121 and 119 pictures), the same model scored 31.40% and 31.69%. [Guth, Ward and McDonnell (2023)](https://doi.org/10.24018/ejeng.2023.8.2.2773) trained three standard architectures on PlantVillage-style laboratory images and tested them on field photographs of the same twelve classes: average accuracy 51.3%, with some classes under 30%. [Noyan (2022)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2206.04374) found a reason. He deleted the leaf and trained a classifier on 8 background pixels from the corners and edges of each PlantVillage image. It reached 49.0% across 38 classes, against a chance level of 2.6%. Whatever differed between the categories' backgrounds, the background alone carried the label.

**Electrical signals from plant stems.** Plant electrophysiology records small voltage changes from electrodes inserted into stems and uses them to infer stress, infestation or nutrient deficit. The field's benchmark studies have repeatedly split continuous recordings into short windows and divided the windows at random. [Chatterjee and colleagues (2015)](https://doi.org/10.1098/rsif.2014.1225) themselves warned that their stimulus groups were separable by baseline offset before any stimulus was applied, "due to the already separated background information", and tried to correct for it. [Bhadra, Chatterjee and Das (2023)](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0285321) later classified three chemical stimuli from about 38,000 windows drawn from the same programme, 94% of them from ozone experiments, using repeated 50:50 random splits of windows with no grouping by plant.

A series of commercial greenhouse studies on tomatoes improved on this by holding out whole plants for testing, but kept a different problem: every plant was recorded through the same calendar sequence, so "normal" and "stressed" labels came from different weeks. In [Najdenovska et al. (2021a)](https://doi.org/10.3390/app11041414) the normal state was days −2 and −1 before spider-mite infestation and the stressed state was days 10 and 11 after it; eight uninfested control plants were recorded but excluded from the analysis. [Tran et al. (2024)](https://doi.org/10.1007/s13580-023-00589-w) acknowledge that "the data representing control conditions was not acquired in parallel". A classifier that separates week 1 from week 2 in a shared greenhouse, with ageing electrodes and changing weather, will pass this test whether or not it has learned anything about the plant. The one study in the series that used a random split of windows rather than whole plants ([Tran et al. 2019](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-019-53675-4)) reported 98.5% accuracy for drought; those that held out whole plants reported 78% to 90%.

[de Toledo and colleagues (2024)](https://doi.org/10.1080/15592324.2024.2333144) inserted a reference electrode pair into a dead, dried segment of bean stem and recorded it alongside the live plants. Spectral peaks at 2.5, 5 and 7.5 Hz that appeared in the live recordings appeared in the dead stem too, and the authors attribute them to environmental and equipment noise. In their water-control experiment, the best classifier distinguished before-stimulus from after-stimulus windows at 47% and 44%, which is to say not at all.

**A plant foundation model.** [Kirk and colleagues (2026)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.26597) pretrained a Transformer on more than 4 million electrode-hours from over 1,400 plants of 100 species at ten sites, and read out seven stress indicators. The paper states the limits of its own design. Section 4.4.2 states that no species was recorded at more than one site, so site and species cannot be separated, and that no site was held out from training. Outdoor sites used a 1 Hz battery device on LoRaWAN; indoor and greenhouse sites used a 120 Hz USB device on Wi-Fi. The results carry the marks of that design. The best readout, linear electron flow, which the paper describes as scaling with incident light, reached a within-plant trajectory correlation of 0.707; three others scored worse than a no-skill baseline on standardised error. On species not seen in training and without per-species calibration, agreement (concordance correlation) fell to 0.008 and 0.019 on the two thermal stress indicators, against a no-skill value of zero. And when the pretrained backbone was replaced by a frozen, randomly initialised Transformer that had never been trained on anything, within-plant trajectory correlation changed by only 0.025 and 0.024 (Table 8). [Liu and colleagues (2026)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.09905) show why a random Transformer can do this much: untrained self-attention acts as an adaptive smoother of the input sequence, a result that goes back to [Saxe et al. (2011)](https://icml.cc/2011/papers/551_icmlpaper.pdf) on random-weight networks. What the model learned from 4 million hours is the difference between the pretrained and the random result, and on trajectory correlation that difference is small. The paper reports treated and control plants recorded simultaneously, which is the right design for the labels; what it cannot yet show is transfer to a new site.

**Done properly.** [Khait and colleagues (2023)](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cell.2023.03.009), reporting airborne sounds from stressed plants in Cell, tested every classifier by leaving one whole plant out, and ran three controls: the same plant before treatment, an untreated neighbour of the same species, and a pot of soil with no plant. The physical origin of the sounds has been debated (Nardini, Cochard and Mayr 2024, listed in the sources), but the evaluation design is the one the rest of this article argues for.

## Brains: the clock stands in for the thought

The clearest demonstration of the splitting problem comes from neuroscience. In 2017 [Spampinato and colleagues](https://arxiv.org/abs/1609.00344) reported that a deep network could tell which of 40 object categories a person was looking at from their scalp EEG, with 82.9% accuracy on held-out trials. A 2018 preprint from the same group ([Palazzo et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/1810.10974v1)) pushed the figure to 93.7%; that number was removed before the paper was published.

[Li and colleagues (2021)](https://doi.org/10.1109/TPAMI.2020.2973153) worked out why. The original experiment had shown each subject all 50 images of one class in a single 25-second burst, then the 50 images of the next class, and so on. Trials from the same burst were then divided at random between training and test. EEG drifts slowly over seconds and minutes, so two trials recorded seconds apart share the same baseline voltage, and a classifier that learns the baseline of each burst learns the class label for free. Li et al. re-ran the experiment with the images interleaved at random so that class and clock time were no longer tied together. Accuracy across every model fell to chance, roughly 2% to 4% across models and settings, against a chance level of 2.5% for 40 classes. They then went the other way: keeping the original block design but replacing the real labels with arbitrary ones, one per block, several classifiers scored 95% to 100% on labels that meant nothing.

A later study ([Xu et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.17024), now published in [Biomedical Signal Processing and Control](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.bspc.2025.108783)) made the point with no brain at all. They recorded "EEG" from watermelons, labelled it as if it came from standard experiments, and decoded the labels at 88.7% under a random split, against 24.7% (chance 25%) when the recordings used for training and testing were kept apart. The same mechanism explains why decoders can appear to read visual working memory, perception or attention from MEG when they are reading small, condition-specific eye movements ([Mostert et al. 2018](https://doi.org/10.1523/ENEURO.0401-17.2018); [Quax et al. 2019](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neuroimage.2019.03.069)).

The brain-to-text literature has a different leak. Models that translate EEG into sentences by feeding EEG embeddings into a pretrained language model reported BLEU-1 scores around 40 ([Wang & Ji 2022](https://doi.org/10.1609/aaai.v36i5.20472): 40.1; [Duan et al. 2023, DeWave](https://papers.neurips.cc/paper_files/paper/2023/file/1f2fd23309a5b2d2537d063b29ec1b52-Paper-Conference.pdf): 41.35). [Jo and colleagues](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-025-29587-x) (Scientific Reports, 2026; preprint 2024) showed that these evaluations had used teacher forcing at test time, feeding the model the true previous word at every step, which raised scores, in the authors' words, "at least three times" (calculated from their Table 1, 2.6 to 4.5 times on BLEU-1 across three language models). They also replaced the EEG input with random noise, which gave the same scores: 39.3 for EEG against 39.3 for noise with teacher forcing, and 13.7 against 13.9 without it. Without teacher forcing, the models produced generic repeated phrases unrelated to the recording. The language model was supplying the language; the EEG was supplying nothing the test could detect.

For the newer self-supervised EEG foundation models, the evidence so far is from preprints. One 2026 negative-control study found that five such encoders could identify which dataset a recording came from with AUROC 1.000, before and after dimensionality reduction ([Zare 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.24519)); another found that six of seven models leaned on broadband spectral slope, which tracks age as well as pathology ([Bindra & Panwar 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.08583)). Neither has yet been peer reviewed.

## Clinics: the hospital stands in for the disease

In medicine the failure has been measured repeatedly, because clinical models get tested on data from other hospitals.

**Chest X-rays.** [Zech and colleagues (2018)](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pmed.1002683) trained pneumonia detectors on radiographs from two hospital systems and tested them on a third. A separately trained network could tell which hospital system a radiograph came from for 99.95% of images from one system and 99.98% from another, and could identify inpatient films with 100% accuracy. Pneumonia prevalence differed between departments, so the hospital was a usable proxy, and performance fell when the models were tested elsewhere. [DeGrave, Janizek and Lee (2021)](https://doi.org/10.1038/s42256-021-00338-7) found the same in COVID-19 X-ray classifiers: models trained on either of two datasets scored far higher internally than on an external hospital system (external AUC 0.76 and 0.70; the authors describe half the predictive performance as lost), and saliency maps pointed at laterality markers, arrows, image borders and shoulder position rather than lungs. When they cut the laterality marker out of a COVID-positive image and pasted it into a negative one, the predicted odds of COVID rose; the reverse swap lowered them. [Roberts and colleagues (2021)](https://doi.org/10.1038/s42256-021-00307-0) reviewed 62 published machine-learning studies for COVID-19 detection from X-rays and CT scans and judged none of them to be of potential clinical use, because of methodological flaws or underlying biases.

**Skin lesions.** [Winkler and colleagues (2019)](https://doi.org/10.1001/jamadermatol.2019.1735) showed a melanoma classifier the same benign moles with and without the purple surgical skin markings that dermatologists draw around suspicious lesions. Specificity fell from 84.1% to 45.8% when the markings were present. The pen mark was correlated with suspicion, and the model had learned the pen.

**Pathology slides.** [Howard and colleagues (2021)](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-021-24698-1) found that deep networks could identify which hospital had submitted a tissue slide to The Cancer Genome Atlas with one-versus-rest AUROC between 0.964 and 0.998, from differences in fixation, staining and scanning. Stage and survival vary between hospitals, so models predicting outcome from slides can learn the hospital. Standard stain-normalisation methods (Reinhard, Macenko) reduced but did not remove the signature; site prediction stayed above 0.85 AUROC with every method tested. Two preprints report that the current generation of pathology foundation models still carry it (Kömen et al. 2024, listed in the sources; and de Jong et al. 2025): in [de Jong, Marcus and Teuwen (2025)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2501.18055), a logistic regression on frozen embeddings predicted the medical centre of origin with accuracy 0.93 to 0.99 across ten models, more accurately than it predicted the cancer type.

**Hidden subgroups.** [Oakden-Rayner and colleagues (2020)](https://doi.org/10.1145/3368555.3384468) examined a pneumothorax detector with an overall AUC of 0.87 and found that 80% of the pneumothorax cases in the test set already had a chest drain in place. On those cases AUC was 0.94; on cases without a drain, the ones where detection matters, it was 0.77. The model had largely learned to find the treatment.

**Cough recordings.** Early in the pandemic, [Laguarta, Hueto and Subirana (2020)](https://doi.org/10.1109/OJEMB.2020.3026928) reported that an AI could detect COVID-19 from forced coughs recorded on phones with 98.5% sensitivity and 94.2% specificity. [Coppock and colleagues (2021)](<https://doi.org/10.1016/S2589-7500(21)00141-2>) listed seven reasons for caution with crowdsourced respiratory audio, among them that most participants already knew their test result when they recorded, that the recording environment could differ systematically between positives and negatives, and that models might be separating the unwell from the well rather than detecting the virus. The same group then ran the test properly ([Coppock et al. 2024](https://doi.org/10.1038/s42256-023-00773-8)): 67,842 people recruited through the UK's testing programme, 23,514 of them PCR-positive, with a pre-specified analysis plan. Analysed the usual way, the audio classifier reached AUC 0.846. After matching on age, sex and self-reported symptoms, it fell to 0.619, and it offered no improvement over a checklist of self-reported symptoms.

**The cancer microbiome.** In 2020, [Poore and colleagues](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-020-2095-1) reported in Nature that machine learning could distinguish 33 cancer types from the microbial DNA found in 17,625 tumour and blood samples, with most models 95% to 100% accurate. [Gihawi and colleagues (2023)](https://doi.org/10.1128/mbio.01607-23) re-analysed the data and found two separate errors. First, the reference database of bacterial genomes contained fragments of human DNA mislabelled as bacterial, so millions of human reads were counted as bacteria; Gihawi et al. showed the mechanism on one bladder-cancer sample: against a database of bacterial genomes alone, 197,811 reads were called Waddlia; once the human genome was added to the database, 174 were. Second, the normalisation step applied before classification turned read counts of zero into small non-zero values that differed by cancer type: in adrenocortical carcinoma, 71 of 79 samples with zero reads of one virus were all assigned the value 3.078874655. Gihawi et al. then built a matrix of nothing but zeros, ran it through the same normalisation, and trained classifiers that matched or beat the originals on 14 of 32 comparisons. The classifier had been reading a numerical tag stamped on each cancer type by the pipeline. Nature [retracted the paper](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-024-07656-x) in June 2024 with the agreement of all authors, and a dependent Nature Communications paper was [retracted](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-025-64170-y) in September 2025.

In every one of these cases the model was accurate on the data it was given. The problem was what the data allowed it to learn.

## Maps and molecules: the neighbour stands in for the cause

The same failure appears when the shared context is space rather than a site or a session. Things that are close together on a map tend to be alike, so a model tested on points near its training points is rewarded for remembering where it has been.

[Ploton and colleagues (2020)](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-020-18321-y) mapped forest biomass across central Africa from satellite data using a random-forest model trained against a forest inventory of 11.8 million trees. With an ordinary random 10-fold cross-validation the model explained 53% of the variation (R² = 0.53). When test pixels were required to be at least 100 km from any training pixel, the predictive power became "virtually null". A model given only the geographic coordinates of each pixel, with no satellite data at all, performed about as well as the full model. They then re-ran a published global map of soil nematode abundance, built from 73 environmental predictors and reported with R² = 0.86 ([van den Hoogen et al. 2019](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-019-1418-6)), using latitude and longitude as the only predictors, and obtained R² = 0.80. [Fourcade, Besnard and Secondi (2018)](https://doi.org/10.1111/geb.12684) made the point with deliberate absurdity: they built species distribution models for 509 European species using, as the environmental layers, the pixel colours of classical paintings laid over the map of Europe. Most of these models were rated good by the standard evaluation statistics, and some beat models built from real climate and terrain variables, because the paintings, like the species records, are spatially autocorrelated.

Chemistry supplies the cleanest demonstration of a model reading the structure of the dataset rather than the science. [Ahneman and colleagues (2018)](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.aar5169) ran 4,608 reactions (including controls) covering every combination of 15 halides, 4 ligands, 3 bases and 23 additives, described each reaction with 120 computed chemical descriptors, and trained a random forest that predicted reaction yield with R² = 0.92 on a random 30% of the grid. [Chuang and Keiser (2018)](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.aat8603) replaced the 120 chemical descriptors with random numbers, and separately with one-hot codes that merely say which reagent is which, and obtained near-identical performance. On a complete combinatorial grid, a random 70/30 split leaves every reagent represented many times in training, so the task reduces to a lookup of reagent identities, and chemical knowledge adds nothing the test can detect. When the same kinds of model were later tried on reactions from a pharmaceutical company's electronic lab notebooks, where the combinations are whatever chemists happened to run, the best R² was 0.266 and several models were negative ([Saebi et al. 2023](https://doi.org/10.1039/D2SC06041H)).

The common element in all of these is that the test set was drawn from the same structure as the training set, whether that structure is a camera station, a territory, a hospital, a 25-second block, a 100 km neighbourhood or a factorial grid, and the model was credited with learning the subject when it had learned the structure.

## The common thread: pseudoreplication

Ecologists have a name for this, and it predates deep learning by forty years. [Hurlbert (1984)](https://doi.org/10.2307/1942661) defined pseudoreplication as testing for an effect "with data from experiments where either treatments are not replicated (though samples may be) or replicates are not statistically independent", and found it in 27% of 176 ecological field experiments he examined, 48% of those that used inferential statistics. The machine-learning version is the same error with a new instrument. A study records a modest number of animals, plants, patients or sites, cuts each recording into hundreds of clips, windows, slices or pixels, pools them, and divides the pool at random. Clips from the same recording land on both sides, and the test measures whether the model can match two slices of the same session, not whether it can recognise the biology in a new one. In the machine-learning literature the same thing is called leakage; [Kaufman and colleagues (2012)](https://doi.org/10.1145/2382577.2382579) note that the more complex, less described cases are those "where the classical independently and identically distributed (i.i.d.) assumption is violated", which is every case in this article.

The size of the error has been measured in field after field. In ecology, [Roberts and colleagues (2017)](https://doi.org/10.1111/ecog.02881) found in their phylogenetic simulations that random cross-validation and unbuffered leave-one-out chose the correct model structure in only 12% to 18% of runs, and concluded that block cross-validation "is nearly universally more appropriate than random cross-validation" when the goal is prediction to new data. [Kattenborn and colleagues (2022)](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ophoto.2022.100018), mapping tree species from drone imagery, found random hold-outs overestimated performance by up to 28 percentage points relative to spatially blocked ones, and that observations were more alike within one flight than between flights. [Stock, Gregr and Chan (2023)](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41559-023-02162-1) set this out for ecologists in Nature Ecology & Evolution: with spatially autocorrelated data, "models can learn to recognize geographic locations and predict values typical for them, instead of learning the intended relationships between variables".

In wildlife re-identification from photographs, [Adam and colleagues (2025)](https://openaccess.thecvf.com/content/CVPR2025W/FGVC/papers/Adam_WildlifeReID-10k_Wildlife_re-identification_dataset_with_10k_individual_animals_CVPRW_2025_paper.pdf) surveyed the public datasets and found that, with one exception, all of them "either have no default split or use a random split without considering data acquisition details", so that photographs from the same encounter or consecutive video frames sit on both sides. The exception was their own [SeaTurtleID2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2211.10307): 438 turtles photographed over 13 years, where a network scored 83.2% under a random split and 47.6% when test photographs came from different days. In camera trapping, [Schneider and colleagues (2020)](https://doi.org/10.1002/ece3.6147) reported 95.6% at locations seen in training and 68.7% at locations not seen, and [Norman and colleagues (2023)](https://doi.org/10.1111/2041-210X.14031) found within one Bornean survey that an F1-score of 0.76 when training and testing on the same habitat disturbance level fell to about 0.30 on disturbance levels held out.

Medicine has measured it most precisely because patients are the obvious unit. [Saeb and colleagues (2017)](https://doi.org/10.1093/gigascience/gix019) reviewed 62 clinical wearable-sensor papers and found 28 had split by record rather than by person; in their own experiment, record-wise splitting gave 2% error from two subjects' data while subject-wise splitting started at 27%. [Yagis and colleagues (2021)](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-021-01681-w) found that putting different MRI slices of the same patient on both sides of a split added 29 to 55 percentage points of accuracy to brain-disease classifiers; split by patient, their Parkinson's classifiers were at chance. [Tampu, Eklund and Haj-Hosseini (2022)](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41597-022-01618-6) found that in a widely used retinal-scan benchmark, 92% of test images came from patients who were also in the training set. [Badgeley and colleagues (2019)](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41746-019-0105-1) built a hip-fracture detector that reached AUC 0.78 on a random split, then showed the scanner model could be identified from the image with AUC 1.00; on a test set with fracture risk balanced across patient and hospital-process variables, the detector performed at random (AUC 0.52). And in genomics, where the session is the day the samples were processed, [Leek and colleagues (2010)](https://doi.org/10.1038/nrg2825) found that between 32% and 99.5% of measured features tracked processing date across every dataset they examined, and [Soneson, Gerster and Delorenzi (2014)](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0100335) showed on simulated data with no biological signal at all that cross-validation reported strongly inflated accuracy once batch and class were confounded, and that batch correction did not repair the estimate.

**Closed sets.** A second structural problem compounds the first in any monitoring application. Almost all published individual-identification models are evaluated closed-set: every test call is guaranteed to belong to one of the enrolled individuals, so the classifier only has to choose the least unlikely of a handful of candidates. A recorder in the field hears neighbours, floaters, juveniles and migrants that were never enrolled, and a closed-set classifier must give every one of them a known name. The open-set test, in which the system has to reject unfamiliar callers at a stated false-acceptance rate, is the only one that corresponds to monitoring, and the Knight review found it in 6.7% of studies.

**Weak baselines.** A third problem is comparing against the wrong floor. A label-permutation test asks whether a classifier beats random labels; it does not ask whether it beats always guessing the commonest individual, and in unbalanced datasets, where a few dominant animals account for most calls, the permutation threshold can sit below that majority rate. A result that passes the permutation test but not the majority-class rate has not shown that it is better than naming the same bird every time.

## Five checks before believing an accuracy figure

None of these needs a new model or new data. Each one takes the model as it stands and changes only the test.

| Check | What it catches | How to run it | Where it has been done |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1. Split by session, subject or site, never by clip | Clips from one recording, slices from one patient, frames from one encounter, pixels from one camera or neighbours on one map sitting on both sides of the split | Keep whole recording sessions, individuals, cameras, days or spatial blocks together; for maps, buffer test points away from training points. | [Beery et al. 2018](https://arxiv.org/abs/1807.04975); [Roberts et al. 2017](https://doi.org/10.1111/ecog.02881); [Ploton et al. 2020](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-020-18321-y); [Li et al. 2021](https://doi.org/10.1109/TPAMI.2020.2973153); [Adam et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2211.10307); [Saeb et al. 2017](https://doi.org/10.1093/gigascience/gix019) |
| 2. Test the model on the context with the organism removed | Site acoustics, tag or sensor noise, studio backdrop, hospital signature, electrode and equipment artefacts | Run the same classifier on ambient recordings with no animal, background pixels with no leaf, a dead stem, or the scanner and hospital labels alone. Mix one individual's background into another's recordings and see whether the answers follow the background. | [Stowell et al. 2019](https://doi.org/10.1098/rsif.2018.0940); [Noyan 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2206.04374); [Zech et al. 2018](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pmed.1002683); [Badgeley et al. 2019](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41746-019-0105-1); [de Toledo et al. 2024](https://doi.org/10.1080/15592324.2024.2333144); [Tolkova et al. 2026](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pcbi.1013321) |
| 3. Break the link between label and session, site or calendar | Identity or treatment that is the same variable as the recording session, the site or the calendar week | Interleave conditions in random order rather than in blocks; record untreated controls at the same time, in the same room, on the same days; match on the confounders you can measure. | [Li et al. 2021](https://doi.org/10.1109/TPAMI.2020.2973153); [Coppock et al. 2024](https://doi.org/10.1038/s42256-023-00773-8); [Khait et al. 2023](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cell.2023.03.009); [Kirk et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.26597) |
| 4. Run a placebo input and the trivial baselines | Pretrained priors supplying the answer, untrained networks smoothing their way to a correlation, a lookup of identities on a combinatorial grid, the majority class | Replace the input with random noise, one-hot identity codes or geographic coordinates; replace the pretrained backbone with a frozen random one; report chance, the majority-class rate and a label-permutation test together. | [Chuang & Keiser 2018](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.aat8603); [Fourcade et al. 2018](https://doi.org/10.1111/geb.12684); [Jo et al. 2026](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-025-29587-x); [Kirk et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.26597); [Xu et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.17024); [Soneson et al. 2014](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0100335) |
| 5. Test open-set when the system will meet strangers | Closed-set inflation, and false acceptance of individuals never enrolled | Hold some individuals out entirely, set the acceptance threshold without looking at them, and report how many of them the system wrongly accepts as known. | [Stowell 2022](https://doi.org/10.7717/peerj.13152); [Knight et al. 2024](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.tree.2024.05.007) |

The field's own guidance now says the same. The 2026 practitioner guide for deep learning on animal sound ([Ghani et al. 2026](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pcbi.1014604)) instructs readers to avoid random splits and to test on sites or periods not seen in training; the 2025 Biological Reviews guide to automatic detection ([Kershenbaum et al. 2025](https://doi.org/10.1111/brv.13155)) says an effective test set may be built from a separate microphone, time frame, location or individual, and notes that "little to no standards on the best approaches exist".

For anyone evaluating a biological AI model, the practical summary is short. Ask what the unit of splitting was. Ask whether the context alone can predict the label. Ask what the model scores against a random input, a frozen random network, and the majority class. Ask whether it was tested with strangers present. If the paper cannot answer, the accuracy figure measures the test rather than the organism.
